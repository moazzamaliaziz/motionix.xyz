Printing requires higher resolution than screens. A photo that looks sharp on your monitor might print blurry. Here's how to prepare images for print.

## DPI explained

**DPI (Dots Per Inch)** measures print resolution:
- **72 DPI:** Screen standard. Looks fine on monitors, blurry in print.
- **150 DPI:** Acceptable for large prints viewed from a distance (posters, banners).
- **300 DPI:** Professional standard. Sharp at normal viewing distance.

## Print size calculations

To find the pixel dimensions needed for a print:

```
Width in inches × DPI = pixels needed
8 inches × 300 DPI = 2400 pixels wide
```

## Common print sizes

| Print size | Pixels at 300 DPI | Pixels at 150 DPI |
|-----------|-------------------|-------------------|
| 4×6 inches | 1200×1800 | 600×900 |
| 5×7 inches | 1500×2100 | 750×1050 |
| 8×10 inches | 2400×3000 | 1200×1500 |
| A4 (8.3×11.7) | 2490×3510 | 1245×1755 |
| Letter (8.5×11) | 2550×3300 | 1275×1650 |

## Resizing for print

1. **Check the original resolution.** If the image is 1200×1800 pixels, it's good for a 4×6 print at 300 DPI.
2. **Resize to the target dimensions.** Don't upscale — it creates softness.
3. **Set the DPI metadata.** Some print services read DPI metadata.
4. **Save as JPEG at quality 90+.** Print needs higher quality than screen.

## When upscaling is necessary

If you need a larger print than the resolution supports:
- **AI upscaling** can increase resolution by 2-4x with reasonable quality.
- **Accept lower DPI.** A 200 DPI print is acceptable for most viewing distances.
- **Viewing distance matters.** A poster viewed from 6 feet looks fine at 150 DPI.

## Common mistakes

- **Using screen-resolution images for print.** 72 DPI prints look blurry.
- **Over-compressing.** JPEG artifacts are much more visible in print than on screen.
- **Ignoring aspect ratio.** Resizing distorts the image if proportions aren't maintained.
